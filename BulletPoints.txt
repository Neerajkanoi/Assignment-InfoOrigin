I started by looking at the raw CSV first, checking the first few rows and 
the total number of rows so I could understand the data before doing any analysis. I then checked how many rows
had different values for v2_total and v1_total. There were 16,000 out of 20,000 rows. At first this looked like 
a big number, but it was not very useful by itself because the assignment already mentioned that 
rounding differences and the change in books could affect many rows.

Next, I looked at the average, minimum, and maximum difference between v2 and v1. 
Most of the differences were very small, usually only a cent or two. However, the biggest 
difference was more than $34. This showed that while most orders were fine, there was a smaller
 group with much larger errors.

I then looked at the differences by category. Four categories looked completely normal, with 
differences of no more than a couple of cents. Books had a consistent difference, which made 
sense because their prices had changed. Fragile orders were different because most were fine, 
but some had errors of several dollars.

At first, I thought the problem with fragile orders might be related to weight. Heavier orders
 seemed like a possible reason for a pricing error, so I checked the relationship between 
 weight and the size of the difference. There was almost no connection, so I ruled that idea out.

After that, I split the fragile orders into express and non express. This is where the 
main problem became clear. Every non express fragile order was clean, while every express fragile 
order was overcharged. There were no exceptions, which showed that express was the main trigger for the issue.



The small differences of a cent or two were just normal rounding differences.
